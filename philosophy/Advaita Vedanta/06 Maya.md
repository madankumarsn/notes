# Step 6 — Maya

*Tradition: Advaita Vedanta · Texts: Vedanta Sara (Sadananda), Shankara; critic: Ramanuja's Sri Bhashya · Modern interpreter: Swami Vivekananda, Jnana Yoga lectures · Source talk: Maya*

*Note: this step is drawn from the transcript and hasn't yet been discussed in our conversation.*

---

## Where we are

- **Step 5:** the world isn't a second reality — it's Brahman appearing as many.
- But **what** is this appearing? Why does it look so solid, painful and convincing?

Step 6 names it — **Maya** — and looks at it three ways: the classical definition, its critics, and Vivekananda's reframing.

---

## Summary

- **Classically,** Maya is ignorance with two powers — it *hides* reality and *projects* a false one. It's neither real nor unreal.
- **Critics** (Ramanuja) found seven logical problems with this idea.
- **Vivekananda** reframed it: Maya isn't a theory explaining the world — it's a plain **description** of the contradictions we live in every day.

---

## Opening story: Manmatha and Vivekananda

A disciple begged Vivekananda to explain Maya. Vivekananda tried to change the subject; the disciple insisted.

As Vivekananda spoke, the room began to shimmer like air above a fire — the walls, then Vivekananda himself, then the disciple's own body seemed to dissolve. Only the voice remained. He burst out: "All this is Maya — even your work, your mission!"

Then he noticed he'd addressed his guru with the casual, intimate "you" (*tumi*) rather than the respectful one (*apni*). The moment that sense of difference returned — the world snapped back into place.

Vivekananda, smiling: "You're right, all this is Maya. If you don't like this play, go to the Himalayas and merge in Brahman. If you can't, come and help with this work."

**Note:** the sense of difference itself brought the world back.

## Part A — The classical definition

### 6.1 What Maya is, at its core
**Ignorance** (*ajnana*) — not knowing that you are Brahman. Ignorance is the problem; knowledge is the solution (Step 4).

### 6.2 Five characteristics (*Vedanta Sara*)

| # | Sanskrit | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Sad-asad-bhyam anirvachaniya* | Can't be called real or unreal | It disappears on enlightenment (not real); yet we live in it daily (not unreal) |
| 2 | *Trigunatmaka* | Made of the three *gunas* | Sattva (clarity), rajas (activity), tamas (inertia) — borrowed from Samkhya |
| 3 | *Jnana-virodhi* | Removed by knowledge | Ignorance of French is removed only by knowledge of French |
| 4 | *Bhava-rupa* | A positive "something," not a mere absence | It actively hides and projects (see 6.3) |
| 5 | *Yat-kinchit* | An indefinable "something" | It vanishes when examined — like possession by a ghost that leaves once noticed |

**On #1:** One Swami: "On our side of enlightenment, we have the question and no answer. On their side, they have the answer and no question."

**On #5:** Himalayan monks' saying: "Don't try to *establish* ignorance — cut it down."

### 6.3 Two powers

| Power | Sanskrit | Rope-snake | Applied to us |
|---|---|---|---|
| Veiling | *avarana* | Not seeing the rope | Not knowing I am Brahman |
| Projecting | *vikshepa* | Seeing a snake | Seeing a separate, limited world and self |

### Sri Ramakrishna's examples of veiling
| Example | Point |
|---|---|
| Rama, Sita, Lakshmana walking in single file through the forest | Lakshmana (individual self) sees Rama (supreme Self) only when Sita (Maya) steps aside |
| Sun reflected in muddy water | A clouded mind can't reflect the self clearly |
| Pond covered with water hyacinth | Push it aside and you see clear water; let go, and it drifts back — like samsara after a good talk or meditation |

### 6.4 Maya isn't all bad
| Kind | Content | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| *Avidya maya* (of ignorance) | Lust, anger, greed, jealousy, pride | Binds |
| *Vidya maya* (of knowledge) | Discrimination, dispassion, devotion | Leads out |

- Ramakrishna: the cobra carries poison that kills others but doesn't harm itself. God wields Maya without being bound by it.
- Vivekananda: you can't escape the machine — learn to work it, and it sets you free.

## Part B — The critique: Ramanuja's seven objections

**Context:** After Shankara, dualist schools attacked Advaita for ~500 years, focusing on Maya. Ramanuja (founder of qualified non-dualism, *Vishishtadvaita*) first restated Advaita fairly — to its own followers' satisfaction — and then pressed seven "untenables" (*anupapatti*):

| # | Objection | The question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Locus (*ashraya*) | Where is ignorance? Not in Brahman (darkness in the sun?). Not in the individual (who only exists *because of* ignorance). |
| 2 | Nature (*svarupa*) | What *is* it — real or unreal? |
| 3 | Indefinability (*anirvachaniya*) | "Neither real nor unreal" violates the law of the excluded middle. |
| 4 | Concealment (*tirodhana*) | How can self-luminous Brahman be hidden? Like closing your eyes and saying the sun has gone. |
| 5 | Positivity (*bhava-rupa*) | Ignorance is just absence of knowledge. What's a "positive" not-knowing? |
| 6 | Remover (*nivartaka*) | Knowledge of a quality-less Brahman is impossible — everything known has qualities. |
| 7 | Removal (*nivritti*) | A beginningless, positive Maya could never end. |

- Swami: read these in full and Advaita seems to crumble — but there are strong replies. (See John Grimes, *The Seven Great Untenables*.)
- Facing objections deepens understanding: if your position survives attack, you understand it better.

### The debate custom worth noting
Before replying, you had to restate your opponent's view **to their satisfaction**. Swami: much quarreling would disappear if we did this.

## Part C — Vivekananda's reframing: Maya as a statement of fact

### 6.5 The key shift
> **Maya isn't a theory to explain the world. It's simply a statement of fact — a description of the contradictory, paradoxical nature of life.**

The common Indian saying "all is Maya" is, he said, as good a statement of things as you'll find.

### 6.6 "And this is Maya" — examples from life

| Contradiction | Example |
|---|---|
| Death and clinging | Everything dies — cities, mountains, planets; saints and sinners, kings and beggars. Yet we cling to life. Becker's *Denial of Death*: our projects (families, businesses, empires, books) are "immortality projects." Woody Allen: he wanted immortality not through his work but by not dying. |
| Limits of knowledge | We feel we can know everything, then run into endless time, space and causation. 20th-century physics and math: relativity, uncertainty, incompleteness — the very words used to describe Maya. |
| Sense pleasure | Pleasure promises satisfaction, gives a little, leaves us hungrier — yet we return like moths to a flame. |
| Free will | Every breath says "I am free"; investigation (science, religion, neuroscience) keeps saying we aren't. |
| Social progress | Feudalism → capitalism → socialism: we redistribute misery, like rheumatism moving from one joint to another. |
| Enjoyment and suffering | Refinement raises the capacity for enjoyment — and for suffering even more. A dog enjoys food fully and suffers fully. Over-protected children, never exposed to dirt or failure, become more fragile. |
| Attachment | A mother devoted to a son who abuses her; she knows it isn't really love, yet can't let go. |
| Life's drama | We arrive full of hope, grow older and bitter, and are swept off the stage — "victorious or defeated? We don't know." The same house; other children playing in the same garden. |

### Example: Narada's thirst
Narada asks Krishna what Maya is. Krishna asks him first to fetch water. At the village well, Narada meets a young woman, falls in love, marries, has three children, runs the family business. Twelve years later a flood sweeps away his whole family. He wakes on the riverbank, weeping — and Krishna asks: "Where have you been? I've been waiting half an hour."

### Example: the monk at Dakshineswar
A reclusive monk never left his room — until monsoon clouds blackened the sky and then blew away. He came out laughing and dancing. Asked why: "The Maya of the world is just like this. Clear sky, dark clouds, clear sky again."

## Part D — Two objections to seeking beyond Maya, and the replies

### 6.7 Objection 1: Hegel — "the Absolute is progressively perfecting itself in the world"
- The Absolute expresses itself in higher and higher forms until it becomes perfect. Why transcend the world?
- (Swami notes possible Upanishadic influence on Hegel — a contested scholarly question.)
- **Vivekananda's reply:** every expression is a **limitation**. Any expression in time, space and causation is finite. The infinite can never be fully expressed as a finite thing — only imitated.
- **The alternative:** not outward expansion (*pravritti*) but turning back inward (*nivritti*). Peace is found in the subject, not the object. Hence renunciation as the basis of spirituality.

### 6.8 Objection 2: agnosticism — "just live this life, forget the beyond"
- **Vivekananda's reply:** it leaves out the *ideal component* of life. Every human pushes beyond what they have — religion, art, science all reach for more. A snapshot of life minus that urge won't satisfy.
- That urge is the **quest for freedom** — "not this, not this" (*neti neti*).

## Part E — The stages of freedom from Maya

How human thought about freedom develops (Vivekananda):

| Stage | Idea | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Many gods — personified freedom from our limits | Gods of thunder, sun, rain |
| 2 | One God beyond Maya, creator of the universe | Abrahamic religions, Indian theistic schools — dualistic Vedanta begins here |
| 3 | God both beyond and **within** the universe | Spinoza; qualified non-dualism (Vishishtadvaita) — "all this is Brahman" |
| 4 | God is **you** | Advaita — *aham brahmasmi*, *tat tvam asi* |

### Example: Mary Hale's correction
She wrote that Vivekananda taught "all is God." He replied: he never taught that. What he meant: **all is *not* — God alone *is*.**

### The final turn
- What did Maya hide? **You.** Maya didn't hide the truth from you — you *are* the truth.
- So Maya's play was your own play.
- Standing in that light, the same world is seen differently — you are the stage on which the drama is played.

## How Step 6 completes the sequence

| Step | Established |
|---|---|
| 1 | Awareness is distinct from objects |
| 2 | Awareness persists across all states |
| 3 | Awareness is eternal, real, all-pervading, not an object, non-doer |
| 4 | Ignorance and error hide this; knowledge removes them |
| 5 | Objects aren't separate — Atman = Brahman |
| 6 | The appearing itself is Maya: indefinable, contradictory, and seen through by knowledge |

**Connections:**
- Maya's **veiling** = Step 4's ignorance. Maya's **projecting** = Step 4's error.
- Maya's **indefinability** = Step 5's *mithya* (inquirer's view).
- Maya's **contradictions** = why the world never fully satisfies — what drives the search in the first place.

## Check against experience

Pick one of Vivekananda's contradictions (6.6) that you've actually lived — say, freedom vs. determinism, or enjoyment raising the capacity for suffering.

Does calling it "Maya" **explain** it, or just **name** it honestly? Vivekananda says the second — and that's enough.

---

## Contested points

1. **Ramanuja's seven objections** — especially #1 (locus) and #3 (excluded middle). Worth studying with the Advaita replies.
2. **Vivekananda's reframing** dodges the logical problems by refusing to make Maya a theory. Is that a strength or an evasion?
3. **The Hegel critique** — whether "expression is always limitation" is true, or assumes what it needs to prove.

## Where the sequence leads

The conceptual structure is now complete. What remains, by Advaita's own account, is the practice of Step 4:

**hearing → reflection → contemplation**, until "I understand" becomes "I'm convinced," and "I'm convinced" becomes "I see."
